This page is the design for keeping a company's data on its boxes the way a
swarm keeps a torrent: each piece on some of the boxes, spread to save space,
and pulled from all of them at once by a box that takes over. A box is any host
claimed into a company, on any hardware, by a pairing code or a connection file
alike. The plane makes one box the service box and the rest standbys, as
[Kim mini](/docs/kim-mini#more-than-one-box) describes.

## What a piece is

Everything kept off a box is already cut into sealed pieces in the hosted store,
the `box_backup` bucket, under the company's folder:

| Folder | What its pieces are |
|---|---|
| `snapshots/`, `chunks/` | snapshot manifests and the chunks they name |
| `base/`, `base-chunks/` | base backups of the database and their chunks |
| `wal/<system>/`, `wal-history/<system>/` | WAL pieces named by timeline and LSN range, and timeline histories |
| `file-journal/`, `file-chunks/` | file journal entries and the file contents they name |

A manifest lists the chunks of each archive member
(`internal/offsite/snapshot.go`). A chunk is cut by its content, typically
2 MiB, and named by an HMAC of its plaintext under a key derived from the
company key, so the same bytes always get the same name. Each piece is sealed
to the company key with HPKE, binding the company, the piece's name and its kind
(`internal/box/sealed_stream.go`). Every box of the company may read the
folder, and only the service box may write it
(`supabase/migrations/20261112000001_a_box_reads_its_backups_by_their_folder.sql`).

The store holds every piece. A standby holds only the newest snapshot's chunks,
in `/var/lib/internkim-backups/standby-chunks`, so a take-over fetches the base
backup, the WAL and the file journal from the store after the service box has
failed (`cmd/internkim-host/take_over.go`).

## The rule

Every piece is held by at least `copies` seeds. A seed is the hosted store or a
standby holding the company key. The default is two: the store and one standby.
`copies` is a company setting in the record beside the take-over lapse in
`box_take_over`, set in company setup from two up to the number of seeds.

With two copies the company survives losing any one box together with the
store. If the service box and the store go, a standby holds each piece. If a
standby and the store go, the service box still holds the live company and
seeds it again on whatever seed is left or comes back; only history the lost
standby alone held is gone.

The store stays the seed of last resort: it holds every piece, sealed, and a box
fetches from it only what no reachable box holds. A company with one box relies
on it alone.

## The boundary

The service box always holds the complete live database and workspace, since
PostgreSQL cannot run on part of its data, and is no seed. Seeding covers what
standbys hold: snapshots, base backups, WAL pieces, file journal entries, and
the history kept, eight snapshots and two base backups by default. At take-over
the new service box gathers the pieces it lacks and becomes complete; its share
stops counting, and what it held is seeded again on the boxes that remain.

## Placement

A piece's seeds are the standbys that rank first for it by weighted rendezvous
score, weighted by `box.weight`, the kind of score the record already uses to
choose which standby takes over
(`supabase/migrations/20261021000002_a_lapsed_box_is_taken_over_by_the_freshest_standby.sql`).
Every box computes its share from the list of its company's boxes, so no table
assigns pieces, and a box that joins or leaves moves only the pieces it ranks
first for. A box joining a company of one box ranks first for everything and
becomes a full clone.

Replication puts whole pieces on `copies − 1` standbys. Erasure coding cuts
each group of k pieces into n fragments, any k of which rebuild the group. For a
company keeping 100 GB of sealed pieces, each standby holds:

| Boxes | Standbys | One copy on the boxes | Two copies on the boxes | Erasure coded, one standby may be missing |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2 | 1 | 100 GB | impossible | impossible |
| 3 | 2 | 50 GB | 100 GB | 100 GB, since 1 of 2 is replication |
| 5 | 4 | 25 GB | 50 GB | 33 GB as 3 of 4 |

The store holds its own 100 GB on top.

The recommendation is replication. Erasure coding saves space only from four
standbys on, against a second copy the rule does not ask for, and costs fixed
stripes over chunks of varying size, re-encoding whenever a standby joins or
leaves, and k fetches per chunk. A company that wants more sets `copies` to
three.

## Discovery and transfer

The plane is the tracker. A box reports each piece it has verified as a row
naming the box and the piece's path, and the record answers which pieces are
below their count and who holds a piece.

A piece needs no trust in the box it came from. Opening it fails if it was
altered or swapped, because the seal binds the name. A chunk's name is then
checked against the HMAC of its contents, and a WAL piece's length against the
range its name carries.

Boxes accept no connection from other networks
([Architecture](/docs/architecture#why-the-host-has-no-inbound-port)), so pieces
move two ways:

- Between networks, through the store. The service box writes each piece there
  once and each seed pulls its share, so the office line carries one upload
  however many boxes there are.
- Within one network, directly. A box serves sealed pieces by name, read-only,
  on its LAN address, to requests signed by another box of its company, and its
  check-in carries that address beside its site. A take-over pulls from every
  box on its network in parallel and the rest from the store, as
  `offsite.FetchAhead` pulls from the store.

The service box keeps each sealed piece it writes until a standby holds it, so
a standby on its network can take a piece before the upload finishes.

A box's rows stop counting once it has gone unseen for the take-over lapse, or
is disconnected or replaced. Its pieces fall below their count, and the next
standby by score pulls them. Pieces no kept manifest names are dropped, as
`ChunkCache.forgetAllBut` drops an older snapshot's chunks.

## What it closes and what it leaves

- The window before the first base backup closes. Kept WAL is useless until a
  base backup gives it a start, and a take-over in that window falls back to
  the newest snapshot's dumps (`internal/companyhost/restore_from_kept_wal.go`).
  The first base backup and the first full file journal entry cross the office
  line whole, hours for a large database. A standby on the same network holds
  them in the time a copy across the room takes.
- Take-over gets faster. The new service box holds its share of the base backup
  and up to 750 WAL pieces before the old one fails, and pulls the rest from its
  peers in parallel.
- Each standby holds its share, a quarter of the pieces with five boxes.

Two things stay separate work. Fencing keeps a service box that lost its place
from acting on anything. A caught-up handover lets a deliberate move bring the
new box level before the old one stops, where Make this box serve stops the old
box first and costs the time a take-over takes.

## Pull requests

1. The tracker: a holdings table where a box writes only its own rows, the
   `copies` setting, and a function naming pieces below their count.
2. A standby keeps its share: `ChunkCache.KeepUp` widens from the newest
   snapshot to every folder, ranks itself per piece, verifies and reports,
   pulling from the store.
3. A take-over reads what it holds before asking the store.
4. Re-seeding after a box goes unseen or is removed.
5. Transfer within one network: the signed read-only piece server, the LAN
   address in the check-in, and parallel pulls from peers.
6. The first base backup at LAN speed: the service box keeps sealed pieces until
   a standby holds them.

Each changes the pages describing what it changes,
[Kim mini](/docs/kim-mini) and [What lives where](/docs/what-lives-where) first.

## Open questions

1. May a box listen on its LAN, ending "every arrow points outward" for peers on
   one network? Recommendation: yes, bound to the LAN address, only while
   another box of the company shares its site, answering only box-signed
   requests for sealed pieces. Without it steps 5 and 6 drop out and 1 to 4
   stand.
2. May a standby on the service box's site acknowledge a write, so writes go on
   through an internet outage? Recommendation: no. A write counts once a seed on
   another site holds its piece, which is the store unless a standby's site
   differs. One building's power cut should cost no write.
3. Should the standby first in line to take over hold everything?
   Recommendation: no. Spreading with parallel pulls is what saves space, and
   `copies` of three serves a company that wants more.
4. Should the store drop pieces the boxes hold enough of? Recommendation: no. A
   company with one box relies on it, and it is the one seed every box reaches
   from any network.
