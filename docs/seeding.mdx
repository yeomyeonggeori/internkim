This page is the design for keeping a company's data on its boxes the way a
swarm keeps a torrent: each piece on some of the boxes, spread to save space,
and pulled from all of them at once by a box that takes over.
[Kim mini](/docs/kim-mini#more-than-one-box) says what a box, the service box
and a standby are.

## The rule

The service box holds the company live, the hosted store holds its whole log,
and the standbys hold the log once more between them, each piece on one standby.

The rule has no setting and treats no number of boxes differently. A company on
one box is in two places. A standby makes three, and any two can go with nothing
saved lost.

| Lost | What remains |
|---|---|
| The service box and the store | The standbys hold the log whole |
| The store and a standby | The service box holds the company live |
| The service box and a standby | The store holds the log whole |

With the store gone, standbys on different networks cannot reach each other,
and their shares meet when it returns or the boxes share a network.

The service box counts as a place and holds no share of the log. It is the one
box missing at every take-over, so a piece placed there would be absent exactly
when it is read, and it sends each piece to the store without keeping it
(`internal/walstream/kept_pieces.go`).

## One log for each kind of state

| State | Its log | Folders in `box_backup` |
|---|---|---|
| The databases | A base backup, then WAL | `base/`, `base-chunks/`, `wal/&lt;system&gt;/`, `wal-history/&lt;system&gt;/` |
| The files | A full entry, then an entry for each change | `file-journal/`, `file-chunks/` |

A base backup and a file entry are cut into chunks by content, typically 2 MiB
(`internal/offsite/chunker.go`). Every piece is sealed to the company key, and
the seal binds the company, the piece's name and its kind
(`internal/box/sealed_stream.go`), so a box checks a piece by opening it and
needs no trust in the box it came from. Every box of the company may read the
folders and only the service box may write them
(`supabase/migrations/20261112000001_a_box_reads_its_backups_by_their_folder.sql`).

A write counts once the store holds its piece. PostgreSQL waits for the WAL
follower, which acknowledges a position only after its upload
(`internal/walstream/follower.go`), and a reply waits at the file barrier until
its entry is kept (`internal/filestream/barrier.go`). A standby on the service
box's network never stands in for the store here, so one building's power cut
costs no write.

The log starts when a company's first box is claimed, while the database is
empty and its first base backup is small. A box follows whenever it holds the
company key, whatever the number of boxes
(`cmd/internkim-host/kept_off_the_box.go`). A computer that holds no company key
keeps no log, and the file barrier answers it that nothing is journaled.
`company_keeps_a_backup` and the setup page count a base backup as a backup, so
a box can be made to serve as soon as the log has one.

## Placement

A piece belongs to the standby whose public key, hashed with the piece's path,
scores highest among the standbys that hold the company key and were seen
within the take-over lapse. The check-in answers with that list, and every box
computes its share from it and the manifests in the store. No table assigns
pieces, and a box that joins or leaves moves only the pieces it wins. One
standby holds the whole log. Four hold a quarter each.

A box that becomes the service box stops ranking, and its share goes to the
standbys that remain. A box keeps a piece that left its share until the standby
it went to reports holding it. A piece the store's manifests no longer name is
dropped, so the service box's pruning reaches every copy.

## Fetching

A box fetches a piece from a box on its own network that has it, asking in rank
order, and from the store otherwise. The store is the one holder every box
reaches, since boxes accept no connection from other networks
([Architecture](/docs/architecture#why-the-host-has-no-inbound-port)).

A take-over reads its own share from disk and fetches the rest sixteen at a
time (`offsite.FetchAhead`).

To answer its neighbours a box serves sealed pieces by name, read-only, on the
LAN address its check-in carries, to requests signed by another box of its
company.

## What a box reports

At each check a standby reports a digest of the standby list it ranked against,
the base backup its share starts from, and the WAL position and file entry its
share is whole through. Those four values on its `box` row stand where a row
per piece would be 50,000 rows for each 100 GB.

The setup page reads it, and so does a box deciding whether to drop a piece
that left its share. Fetching does without it, so a stale report costs one
request to the store.

## The snapshot goes

Every fifteen minutes the service box dumps both databases and archives every
file root into a snapshot (`companyhost.SnapshotTo`). A take-over that finds
both logs uses neither half: the database comes from the base backup and the
WAL, and the newest full entry empties each root before the journal is replayed
(`internal/companyhost/restore_file_journal.go`). The seven older snapshots
have no reader, since no command fetches one.

Two things ride on the snapshot alone and move first.

1. The roster. `policy.json` is under `/run/internkim`, outside every
   journaled root, and a restore recreates the people's accounts from it before
   files get their owners back (`recreateThePeople`). It joins the file journal.
2. The release and the PostgreSQL major. The snapshot's manifest carries both
   (`hostbackup.Manifest`), and a restore refuses a newer release. The base
   backup's document carries them, and a box on another major refuses before
   anything changes, since WAL replays only on the major that wrote it.
Dumps stay in the daily archive on the box (`internkim-host backup`), the one
form that restores onto another PostgreSQL major.

What is then deleted: the `snapshot` verb, its timer and `SNAPSHOTS_KEPT`;
`Ship`, `Newest`, `Prune`, `KeepUp` and `Materialize` in `internal/offsite`;
the `snapshots/` and `chunks/` folders; the archive `restore --taking-over`
is given; and the fallbacks to a snapshot's dumps and files (`ErrNoKeptWAL`,
`ErrNoFileJournal`). `offsite.Snapshot` stays as the chunk writer both logs
use.

## Order

1. The log starts with the first box.
2. The two things above move, and a take-over starts from the logs alone.
3. The snapshot is deleted, once every company keeps a base backup and a full
   entry.
4. A standby keeps its share and reports it, and a take-over reads its own
   share before asking the store.
5. Shares move when a box joins, leaves, goes unseen or changes role.
6. Boxes on one network answer each other.

Each step changes [Kim mini](/docs/kim-mini) and
[What lives where](/docs/what-lives-where) with it.

## Decisions

1. A company on one box waits on the store for each write, as every other
   company does. People reach a box through the hosted service, and nothing a
   person uses on the box's own network writes: the pairing page only reads,
   and Wi-Fi and the admin password come from the hosted service. While the
   line is down the agent still writes when a schedule falls due or work is in
   flight. Its commits wait without a deadline, each holding one of its 30
   database connections, so its scheduler stops at its next claim and the agent
   carries on from there when the line returns. admind stops waiting after two
   minutes, which leaves that write on the box alone. Mail is fetched only when
   asked and the relay's inbound queue is files, so neither writes to the
   database.
2. A box listens on its LAN, bound to its LAN address, only while another box
   of the company shares its site, and answers only box-signed requests for
   sealed pieces. For boxes on one network this ends "every arrow points
   outward", and [Architecture](/docs/architecture#why-the-host-has-no-inbound-port)
   changes with step 6.
